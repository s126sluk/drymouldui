What should I do before you arrive?

Very little, and the less you do the better.

Please don't scrub, spray or wipe the affected area before we get there. Disturbing growth makes it harder for us to see what we're dealing with and can spread the problem into rooms that were fine.

What does help:

  • Close the room off if you reasonably can, and keep it that way until we arrive.
  • Stop any active water. If there's a live leak, get a plumber to it first — that's a separate trade to ours and it needs to happen before or alongside our works.
  • Clear access. Move small furniture, valuables and anything breakable out of the area if it's safe to do so. Leave the heavy items to us.
  • Take photos of the area as it is now, and note roughly when you first saw it. Useful for your own records.
  • Have the story ready. When you first noticed it, whether there'd been a leak, storm or flood beforehand, and whether anything has been done to it already.
  • Tell us about the household. Pets, small children, anyone in the home with a health sensitivity, and any access constraints — parking, stairs, lift bookings, strata rules or building induction requirements.

Do I need to leave my home during the works?

In most cases, no.

For a single localised area we set up containment around the work zone and work inside it, so the rest of the home stays usable. You'll lose access to that zone while we're in it, and there'll be equipment noise.

You're more likely to need to be out where the works are larger — multiple rooms, materials coming out, or the affected area covers the only bathroom or kitchen. If that applies to your job, we'll tell you before we start, not on the day, and it'll be written into the scope along with how long.

One thing we won't do is make a health call for you. If someone in the household has a medical condition and you want to know whether they should be in the property while works are underway, that's a question for their doctor. We're not qualified to answer it and we won't pretend otherwise.

What actually causes mould in a home?

Moisture. Almost without exception.

Spores are already in the air — in every home in the country, all year round. They only become your problem when they land somewhere with enough moisture to grow on. So the question is never really "where did the mould come from", it's "where is the water coming from".

The usual drivers we find:

  • A quiet leak. Under a sink, behind a shower wall, a roof or gutter issue that only shows itself after heavy rain.
  • Condensation. Warm humid air meeting cold surfaces with nowhere to go. Bathrooms without a working exhaust fan and bedrooms shut up over winter are the classics.
  • A one-off water event. Burst pipe, storm, flood — where the surface looked dry within days but the material underneath wasn't.
  • Rising or lateral damp through walls and slabs.
  • Dead air. Wardrobes on external walls, furniture pushed flat against cold walls, rooms that never get opened.

It's why the first thing we look for is the water, not the growth. The growth is the symptom.

Treated properly

Mould is not a cleaning problem — wiping it away leaves spores behind and it returns within weeks. Our technicians follow a controlled remediation sequence — HEPA vacuum of the surface, then a TGA-listed anti-microbial application. Furniture is protected throughout, and every treated surface is photographed before and after.
We treat the cause — Mould is a symptom — moisture is the disease. Dehumidification dries the materials themselves, not just the air, so the colony doesn't simply return.

Faq

Will the mould come back?

Honest answer: nobody can promise you it won't, and you should be wary of anyone who does.

Mould needs moisture. Where the moisture comes back, growth can come back with it. That's the whole thing in one sentence.

What we do is address what's there now and apply our remediation techniques to the affected materials. What decides whether it returns is the moisture source — a leak that's been properly repaired, waterproofing that's been made good, ventilation that actually moves air, humidity that's kept in a sensible range. Where the source is dealt with, a return in the same spot is far less likely. Where it isn't, it can return regardless of what's been applied to the surface, and that's true of any operator's work, not just ours.

So we'll tell you what we found and what we think is driving the moisture, what sits inside our scope, and what needs another trade. If the driver is something we can't fix, you'll hear that from us plainly rather than after the fact.

What surfaces can be treated?

Broadly, it comes down to how absorbent the material is.

Generally treatable in place — hard and semi-porous surfaces: painted walls and ceilings, tiles and grout, glass, metal, sealed timber, window frames and sills, skirtings, vanities, cabinetry, hard flooring.

Assessed case by case — absorbent materials that have taken water in: carpet and underlay, insulation, plasterboard that's been wet through, particleboard and chipboard, soft furnishings and some untreated timbers. Depending on how far it's gone, these are either treatable in place or recommended for removal. That's a scope conversation we have with you before anything comes out.

Where a surface is within scope, we apply a sanitising agent, a disinfectant or an antimicrobial appropriate to that material, along with the rest of our remediation techniques.

Outside our scope — we don't carry out plumbing repairs, waterproofing, electrical work or the rebuild afterwards.

Why does my place smell musty when I can't see anything?

That smell is worth taking seriously rather than covering with an air freshener.

It's produced by growth that's active somewhere — and if you can't see it, it's usually behind or beneath something. The regular candidates: inside a wall cavity after a slow leak, under carpet and underlay, beneath a vanity or kitchen kickboard, in the subfloor or roof space, inside a wardrobe on an external wall, or in the seal of a washing machine.

Visible growth is only ever part of the picture, which is why an assessment involves moisture readings rather than a walk-around and a look. Materials can read wet well past the point they feel dry to touch.

Can't I just deal with it myself?

Sometimes, yes — and we'd rather tell you that than sell you something you don't need.

A small isolated patch on a hard, non-porous surface — a bit of grout, a painted bathroom wall in a room with no exhaust fan — is often within reach of a homeowner with decent ventilation and the right product.

Where it stops being a DIY job:

  • It keeps coming back in the same spot. That's a moisture source, not a surface problem, and no amount of wiping changes it.
  • It's on or in absorbent materials — plasterboard, carpet, insulation, timber. Surface products don't get into a material that has taken water in. You clear what you can see and leave what you can't.
  • You don't know where the water came from.
  • Someone in the household has a health concern about it. That's a conversation to have with a doctor before you go near it, not after.

On bleach, since it comes up constantly: in our experience it changes the appearance on a hard surface and does very little on a porous one. If a patch comes back looking the same a few weeks later, it was never a surface problem to begin with.

Do I need testing done first?

Not always — and it's worth being clear about what testing is for.

Where there's visible growth and an obvious moisture source, testing often doesn't change what needs to happen. You can see the problem and you know where the water came from.

Where it earns its place is when you need something documented rather than described: a written record of conditions before and after works, a situation where two parties disagree, a purchase decision, or a case where you need evidence rather than an opinion. It also has a role when there's a smell or a symptom and nothing visible — when the real question is whether something is happening somewhere you can't see.

What we won't do is tell you that a result means something about your health. A reading is a reading — interpreting it in relation to a person is a matter for a medical practitioner, not us.
